## The short answer

Most dogs bark at everything for one of seven reasons: alerting, demanding attention, frustration, fear, boredom, play, or separation distress. The fix depends on the cause, so the first step is to identify which of the seven triggers is driving the barking before you change how you respond.

## Why dogs bark: the 7 most common triggers

### 1. Alert barking

Alert barking is a short, sharp burst aimed at a sound or movement: the doorbell, a delivery van, a person walking past the window. The dog stops once the trigger disappears. It is the most common reason owners describe a dog as barking "at everything".

### 2. Demand barking

Demand barking is directed at you. It often starts as a soft woof and escalates until you respond — with food, a walk, or simply eye contact. Every response, including telling the dog off, can accidentally reward it.

### 3. Frustration barking

Frustration barking happens when a dog can see something it wants but cannot reach it — another dog behind a fence, a toy under the sofa, a squirrel in the tree. It tends to be higher pitched and repetitive.

### 4. Fear barking

Fear barking sounds defensive and comes with a lowered or tucked body. The dog is trying to increase distance from something that feels threatening. Punishing this type reliably makes it worse.

### 5. Boredom barking

Boredom barking is monotonous and often happens when a dog is left alone with nothing to do. It is less about a specific trigger and more about a lack of physical and mental exercise.

### 6. Play barking

Short, high-pitched barks mixed into a play bow and loose, bouncy movement are usually play. It is normal, but it can tip into over-arousal if play does not have clear breaks.

### 7. Separation-related barking

Separation barking starts shortly after you leave and often continues for a long time. Neighbours usually hear it before the owner does. It is a distress response, not spite.

## How to respond to each type

Match the response to the trigger rather than treating all barking the same way. For alert barking, manage the environment: close the blinds, use a white-noise machine, and reward the dog the moment it looks at the trigger and then back at you. For demand barking, remove the reward: wait for silence before giving attention, food, or the walk. For frustration and boredom, add enrichment such as food puzzles, sniffing games, and a longer decompression walk. For fear and separation barking, build a plan with a professional rather than improvising.

## What not to do

Shouting "quiet" is the most common mistake. To a dog, shouting sounds like barking back, which can escalate the noise. Electric and spray bark collars suppress the symptom without touching the cause, and they frequently make fear-based barking worse. Neither approach teaches the dog what you actually want, which is to settle.

## When to see a vet or behaviourist

Book a veterinary appointment if the barking started suddenly in an adult dog, if it is paired with pacing, drooling, or destruction, or if your dog is losing sleep. Some medical conditions, including pain and thyroid problems, can present as restlessness and vocalising. If the behaviour is separation-related or fear-based, ask your vet for a referral to a certified applied animal behaviourist.
