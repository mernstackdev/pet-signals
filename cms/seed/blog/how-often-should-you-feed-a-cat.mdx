## The short answer

Most adult cats do best with two or three measured meals a day, at roughly the same times each day. Kittens need three to four smaller meals, senior cats often do better with two to three, and the exact amount depends on the calories in your specific food rather than a fixed scoop size.

## How many meals a day?

### Kittens

Kittens under six months have small stomachs and high energy needs, so offer three to four meals a day. Keep feeding the same food they were weaned onto and change brands gradually over about a week.

### Adult cats

Two meals a day is the baseline for most healthy adult cats; three is fine if your schedule allows. Splitting the daily ration into more, smaller meals reduces the long stretches of hunger that drive begging and counter-surfing.

### Senior cats

Cats over about ten years old often eat better with two to three smaller meals, especially if their sense of smell has dulled. Warm the food slightly and offer it in a quiet, low-traffic spot.

## Free-feeding vs scheduled meals

Free-feeding — leaving dry food out all day — is convenient but makes it almost impossible to know how much your cat is eating. That matters, because a drop in appetite is one of the earliest signs of illness in cats. Scheduled meals give you a daily appetite reading and make weight control far easier.

## How much to feed

Follow the calorie guidance on the food packaging, not the scoop that comes with it, and treat that number as a starting point. Weigh your cat every month and adjust in small steps. A cat at a healthy weight should have a visible waist when viewed from above and ribs you can feel but not see. If the number on the scale drifts, change the portion before changing the food.

## How to switch from free-feeding

Do it gradually over one to two weeks. Pick two fixed meal times, put the food down for about twenty minutes, then lift it. Start by offering slightly more than the daily ration so your cat does not panic, then reduce to the target portion as the routine settles. Expect some protest for the first few days; skipping one meal is not dangerous for a healthy adult cat, but check with your vet first if your cat has a medical condition.

## Signs your feeding routine needs adjusting

Revisit the plan if your cat is losing or gaining weight, if it wakes you at night for food, or if it stops finishing meals. Sudden appetite loss in a cat is a reason to call your vet the same day, because cats can develop serious liver problems after only a couple of days of not eating.
